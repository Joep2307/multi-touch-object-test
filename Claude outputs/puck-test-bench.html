Start here

One number decides most of the leg-height question before you print anything

The table doesn't feel your puck, it measures a capacitance through the glass. That coupling falls off with distance — but glass and air don't count the same. Glass has a dielectric constant around 7, so 3.5 mm of table glass behaves roughly like 0.5 mm of air. Every millimetre of air you add on top of it is worth seven millimetres of glass.

Take the effective distance from a pad to the sensor grid as 0.5 mm + gap, and the signal as roughly proportional to one over that. The prediction:

Leg gapEffective distanceSignal, relative to a foot flat on the glass
0 mm0.50 mm100%contact
0.25 mm0.75 mm67%marginal
0.50 mm1.00 mm50%marginal
1.00 mm1.50 mm33%likely invisible
1.50 mm2.00 mm25%invisible

So the 0 / 0.5 / 1 mm series probably doesn't have a winner — it has a threshold somewhere in it, and the useful answer is where. And the same table read backwards is the real design rule: you want the feet at 0 mm and everything else that conducts — the web joining the feet, the top plate, any metal in a mount — at 1 mm or more, because up there it is already too faint to be mistaken for a touch.

That also puts a number on something the sticker spec assumed the other way round. The spec reasoned that since the 3–4 mm of glass dominates, a 0.2 mm setback of the traces barely helps. On this model 0.2 mm of air is worth 1.4 mm of glass, and cuts the trace signal by about 30%. Worth settling by measurement rather than argument — coupon A does exactly that.

The corollary that saves you a squeaky puck

The foot never touches the sensor — the glass is an insulator, so the contact is capacitive either way. That means you can cover the feet with a thin slip film without paying much: 0.05 mm of hardcoated PET is worth about 0.02 mm of air, a 3% loss. Bare conductive PLA on glass squeaks and can leave grey marks; a PET skin over the foot fixes both for almost nothing. Test it, but expect it to be free.

Method

Three test strips beat twenty-seven pucks

Height × position × material as a full factorial is 27+ prints, each one taking an hour and each one carrying its own warp. Almost every electrical question can be answered instead by a flat coupon: a strip with a row of pads that steps through one variable, a tab on top to hold, and nothing else. Lay it on the table, open Puck diagnose, and read off which pads register.

One print, one thirty-second reading, one variable settled.

Coupon A

Height wedge

Eight identical Ø10 pads, gap stepping 0 · 0.25 · 0.5 · 0.75 · 1.0 · 1.25 · 1.5 · 2.0 mm. Pads at 35 mm pitch so they can't merge — that's a 280 mm strip, so split it into two strips of four.

Reads: the threshold gap, in mm.
Sets: leg height, and how high the web has to sit.

Coupon B

Diameter wedge

Pads at Ø4 · 6 · 8 · 10 · 12 · 14 mm, all flat on the glass. A finger is roughly Ø8–10 mm, which is what the controller is tuned for; smaller pads get dropped, larger ones can look like a palm.

Reads: smallest reliable pad, and where it starts misbehaving.
Sets: foot diameter.

Coupon C

Spacing wedge

Six pairs of Ø10 pads at 10 · 14 · 18 · 22 · 26 · 30 mm centre to centre. Below some distance the controller merges two blobs into one and you lose a leg.

Reads: the merge distance, in mm.
Sets: the minimum leg spacing (currently 25 mm) and how many legs fit on the ring.

Coupon D

Material strip

The same Ø10 pad in each candidate: conductive PLA, copper tape, conductive fabric over 1 mm foam, carbon silicone, aluminium tape. Measure each one's resistance from pad to hold tab with a multimeter first.

Reads: does it register, and at what resistance it stops.
Sets: what the feet are made of.

Keep the coupons honest

Print all coupons in one session with the same nozzle, plate and Z-offset, feet down on a smooth plate — not a textured one. First-layer squish changes the real contact area by more than 0.25 mm of design intent, so if you recalibrate between prints you are measuring your printer, not your puck. No brim under the pads: it leaves a ridge exactly where the pad has to sit flat.

Variables

What else is worth testing, roughly in order of how much it can hurt you

01

Grounding — the one that outranks leg height

A passive puck is only visible because it couples the environment to the glass. How well it does that depends on how much conductive area it has and whether a hand is on it. This is worth testing explicitly, because the answer changes what the puck has to look like:

  • Untouched, on the table — does it register at all, and for how long?
  • One finger on the rim — the normal case.
  • Held by an object on top — the case you are about to create. If the mount doesn't conduct, gripping the object un-grounds the puck.
  • Top plate area — none · Ø30 · Ø50 · full Ø68 ring. More conductive area up top means better untouched detection; find the smallest one that works.

If untouched detection is weak, that is also the likely explanation for the dropouts puckMemory is papering over, and it is fixable in hardware rather than in code.

02

How long a resting puck survives

Capacitive controllers continuously re-baseline: something that doesn't move slowly becomes part of the background. Leave a puck untouched and time how many seconds until its contacts drop. puckMemory only covers 10 s, so if the real number is 30 s you have a design problem no threshold will fix — and the fix is more conductive area, not more code. Do this at the centre and at a corner; edges of a PCAP panel are noisier.

03

Where the legs sit

Angular precision is the whole game for identification, and it comes out of one ratio: an angular error of about noise ÷ radius. At r 34 mm, 1.5 mm of position noise is 2.5° per leg. That is measured against a 12° tolerance with a 3° margin to the runner-up — so noise is what eats the margin, and radius is what protects it.

The catch is that radius has almost no headroom. A Ø10 foot at r 34 leaves exactly 1 mm of rim on each side, between the Ø56 window and the Ø80 edge. Growing the ring means growing the puck or shrinking the window:

Ring radiusAngular noise per leg at 1.5 mmCosts you
28 mm3.1°fits a smaller body, but eats the 3° margin
34 mm2.5°current — Ø80 body, Ø56 window, 1 mm rim clearance
40 mm2.1°Ø90 body, or Ø80 with a Ø48 window

16% better angles for a 12% bigger puck is a poor trade. Attack the noise instead — it comes from contact area, flatness and rocking, which are the next three items.

Also worth a variant each: leg count (four is cheaper in rim space and easier to space out; six adds redundancy but crowds the merge distance from coupon C), and foot shape — a solid disc against a ring or a cross of the same outline. A ring shape has less area but a more stable centroid when the puck tilts.

04

Flatness, and why compliant feet may win by default

An 80 mm FDM disc does not come off the plate flat. Warp of 0.2–0.4 mm across that diameter is ordinary, and it is the same order as the entire leg-height range you set out to test. With five rigid feet on a warped disc, three of them carry the puck and two float — which reads on the table as a puck that keeps dropping to four points and leaning on the ringHoldDeg fallback.

So test rigid against compliant directly: rigid conductive PLA feet versus 1 mm conductive foam or a fabric-covered pad that squashes out the warp. Compliant feet give every leg the same contact area regardless of how flat the print is, which should show up straight away as lower position noise. The counter-risk is that a squashy foot's contact area changes with how hard you press, moving the centroid — so measure noise while somebody is actually leaning on it, not only at rest.

Measure warp too, on each printed body: put it on a flat surface and press each foot in turn. Any rock at all is a reject.

05

Weight, glide and centre of gravity

These stop being cosmetic the moment an object sits on top. Rotating the puck to zoom means a puck that slides when you don't want it to is a broken control.

  • Mass — 30 · 60 · 100 g, tuned with steel washers in the rim. Heavy enough not to skate, light enough to lift with one hand.
  • Glide — bare PLA, PET film over the feet, PTFE tape, felt. Score squeak and check the glass afterwards for marks.
  • Centre of gravity — the tallest and heaviest object you intend to mount, tilted until a foot lifts. That number is your mount's height limit, and it is a hard constraint on the concepts below.
06

Behaviour in company

Everything above is one puck alone on a clean table, which is not the situation the thing is for. Repeat the best variant with: a hand resting on the glass beside it, a second puck 100 mm away, the duo pucks nested, and the puck at each corner of the panel. These are where the identity swaps you already fought once will come back.

Mount

Putting an object on top, without blocking the window or breaking the ground path

Four constraints do most of the deciding. The mount has to leave the Ø56 window clear, or the object-pucks lose the map underneath. It has to lock orientation, because the object's front has to agree with the puck's arrow or rotation becomes meaningless. It has to conduct, so that a hand on the object still grounds the feet. And it has to keep the centre of gravity low enough that the puck doesn't tip and lift a foot.

That leaves the 12 mm rim as the only real estate — the same annulus the feet are in, one layer up.

01

Magnets in the rim

Three Ø6×3 mm neodymium magnets pressed into pockets on the r 34 ring, in the gaps between the feet. The object carries a matching plate or three counter-magnets.

For: instant swap, no wear, tolerant of print inaccuracy. Against: no orientation lock on its own — it will happily sit at any of three positions, or upside down if you get the polarity wrong.

02

Bayonet twist

Three tabs on the object drop through slots in the rim and twist 20° to lock.

For: positive lock, exact orientation, no bought parts. Against: FDM tolerances — you'll need about 0.3 mm clearance and two or three iterations; PLA tabs wear and eventually rattle, and twisting the object also twists the puck on the glass.

03

Three-peg press fit

Ø4 mm pegs on the rim, matching holes in the object.

For: the simplest thing that works, orientation comes free if the three pegs are unevenly spaced. Against: press fits in PLA loosen with repeated use, which is exactly the use case; pulling the object off lifts the puck.

04

Heat-set thread

M4 brass insert, object screws down.

For: strong, reusable, cheap. Against: a screw stops where it stops, so orientation is whatever the thread gives you — it needs a shoulder and a locating pin anyway, at which point the thread is only doing the clamping. Too slow to swap in front of visitors.

05

Card slot in the rim

A 1.5 mm slot across the rim takes a printed card or a laser-cut acrylic silhouette that stands upright.

For: nearly free, readable from across the table, keeps the window clear, and you can make twenty variants on a printer. Against: it is a sign, not an object — no good if the physical thing is the point. Worth building regardless, as the cheap parallel track.

06

Magnets plus one key pin — the one to build first

Concept 01 with a single Ø4 mm pin at the top of the ring, sitting on the direction arrow. The magnets hold, the pin decides orientation, and because the pin is on the arrow an object physically cannot go on facing the wrong way.

Make the mount conductive: seat the magnets on the conductive web, or run a strip of conductive fabric from a magnet pocket to the nearest foot. Then a hand on the object grounds the puck exactly like a hand on the rim — and the metal, sitting 6–8 mm up, is far enough from the glass to be invisible to the table. Then: every object becomes a printed adapter disc with the same three-magnet-plus-one-hole pattern, so the interface is designed once.

Order of work

A route through it that doesn't waste filament

  1. Print the four coupons. One session, one calibration. They cost an evening and they fix leg height, foot diameter, minimum spacing and material — four of the variables — before any puck exists.
  2. Print three or four full pucks using the coupon answers, varying only what a coupon can't test: rigid versus compliant feet, five versus four legs, and top-plate area for the grounding question.
  3. Run the scoresheet on the winner and on the current puck as a control, so you can say what the change actually bought.
  4. Then the mount, on the winning body: magnets plus key pin, one adapter disc, two test objects — one light and flat, one tall and heavy — to find the tip limit.

If you want the numbers instead of impressions, the diagnose screen could log per-frame contact positions to a file with the variant code attached, so the noise figure comes out as a measurement rather than a judgement. Say the word and I'll wire that into the existing ?test hooks.

Model caveat: the coupling figures come from a first-order parallel-plate estimate — effective distance is glass thickness ÷ 7, plus the air gap. Real fringing fields and the controller's own thresholds will shift the numbers. It is meant to tell you what to expect from coupon A, not to replace it.
